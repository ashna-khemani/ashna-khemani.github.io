# Storytime
Because every recipe article starts with one.

## the humbling chem exam
I got a 42% on what I think was my first-ever exam at Penn. It was Gen Chem 1, or CHEM 1012. I was very sad. Very embarrassed (almost 2SD below the mean... ow). My life was Joever.

I still remember the feeling of sitting in that exam omg. It was foul. I remember looking at it, and just freezing. The exam was one of those where you either know it or you don't; if it doesn't click for you right away, time is your biggest enemy and you probably will not finish the exam. Which, to me at the time, was the scariest fucking thought in the whole wide world (and still is in some ways).

But yeah,... I was just staring at this paper and putting down mad ravings on it...

## switchin' up the strat
Anyway! Point is, it showed me that I needed to have a better study plan than just reviewing slides, example problems, and the HW's. Reviewing class material may have been enough in high school, but college required a significant amount of self-review, assessment, and relearning. Why? **Because some lectures are honestly pretty useless if you want to learn how to *apply* concepts.** Great for gaining an appreciation and maybe stretching your thinking, but not at all good for a first-time viewer (not always the case, but you're more guaranteed to succeed if you start with this assumption). 

For the next few exams, I realized I had to actually understand the material, not just the problems and patterns. So I cracked open the textbook. And *so* much more was illuminated. I found my own practice problems. If there were things that I forgot about/got wrong on those, I had a little note on my iPad with little reminders... basically a cheat sheet. 

Maybe a more efficient way to learn: make that cheat sheet as I'm reading the textbook. But only the essence of what I read. I should be able to get the info I need with just a glance. Then, once I'd done that for a chapter/concept, I'd do a few problems with the cheat sheet I'd made, and make any minor adjustments (often not needed because I made it while reading). Then when all material was covered, solve a set of practice problems that mixed all concepts, using the cheat sheet as needed but as little as possible.

Eventually, I got pretty goated. Perhaps my dad was right about reading the textbook all along.

UNTIL...

## sike! it probably still won't work!
I suddenly was taking classes that either had no textbook or extremely crappy ones. What the heck?! What was I supposed to study from bruh.

Then the OG strat of running tons of practice problems came in clutch, along with frequenting office hours. I found that I learned best when having conversations...

And there were a ton of other neat little habits that helped me feel good going into an exam. Whether or not it helped me slay,,, that depends hahaha. But hey, I think having the confidence is half the battle for me anyway.

# The Master Recipe
**So anyway here are the things I like to do to study**. 

## Key ingredients
I don't know a lot of people who like these as much as I do, and I kinda just... discovered them through a lot of effort, iteration, time, and reflection.
* **Structure**. Organize information into broader topics/buckets. Understand if/what connections exist between them.
* **Schedule**. What parts will you study when?
* **Hearing > just reading**. Not like. The Voices in your head. But like talking out loud to myself, or better yet, talking to a TA in office hours.
* **Do it**. Solve the problems. Put the theory in motion.


## Steps to slay
1. **Skim through lecture/recitation notes** (maybe slides if my notes suck) to structure a study guide
	* Note topics, terms, formulas to look out for
	* So we remember what we need to understand in detail
2. Use this overview to make a study **schedule**! Lock in on just 1-2 topics (ideally) to understand the "guts" of each topic. **Populate the study guide**
	* Refer to lecture notes if they're good, textbook if it's good, or find a playlist on YouTube
	* Note any points of confusion, anything your ≤95% sure of. Set aside time to ask about it in office hours
		* I personally don't like asking about theoretical questions on online forums because reading is a weaker medium for me. I like talking face-to-face
3. **Practice** problems
	* Do suggested review problems first, if provided for this exam. Otherwise, redo homework problems
	* I usually don't do lecture examples because they're either super simple or too theoretical. But sometimes I'll refer to simple examples to ensure my method aligns.
	* Definitely also do practice exams. Sounds stupid but getting a feel of the paper format itself helps
4. Make cheat sheet for exam if allowed one. Handwrite with a mechanical pencil on printer paper
	* because the Fat Ass Study Guide will most likely be too long :)

So we can see it kind of revolves around making a really good study guide. In some ways, I think it follows the Feynman technique, where you learn by teaching. Except in this case, instead of teaching a peer, I pretend I'm teaching Future Ashna this material. Works out because I often do refer to some of these study guides years later! And making it helps structure my understanding and learn the material in the moment for my exam. It's a win-win!

And possibly a win for others, too. Usually if you start studying early enough and get a grasp on the material before your friends, you can and probably will explain some stuff to them. So more Feynman and practice for you while helpin' out the squad.
