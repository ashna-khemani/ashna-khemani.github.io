# Buying into the scene

* “Magic if” – what if I was meeting a relative after many years who is a threat to my status as the family heir, and turns out they are a bum by family standards? What are things that go through my head – disgust, a desire to mock, worry that the bloodline is cooked, pity that they haven’t figured out life yet by my definition. Use this to inform actioning, and general theme in two takes midterm. (_How the Method Made Acting Modern_)
* Keeping in mind the idea to use imagination, not by own experiences to build this out (_How the Method Made Acting Modern_)
* Helpful to consistently be reminded of considering the given circumstances and the moment before

# Adopting character intentions
* Actioning I think intends to help with this. But I have found it hard to come up with the exact words to describe what I want to _do_ _to_ another character. My instinct is to think more generally about what I want, and how I react to new info from the other character, or my own train of thought develops
* Felt more of this impact from pinpointing the new information we learn in each scene. This approach sends me down the path of pinpointing what information my character picks up on the most in each _line_, and how that would affect them.
* Narration exercise helps flesh out my interpretation of a character and figure out what information resonates with them the most. Helps me find a place to take a beat to absorb information, or let a very specific thought form in my head. Identify a trigger word in the line before that makes it land and motivates my character to take the next action (Goldman, _Cutting Room Floor_). This also helps address feedback where we “listen more than we act”
* Milch's point about ambivalence in *Life's Work* also resonated with me, and I remember touching on it in class with "Greenleaf" (ex: Kerissa wants to put Grace down, but deep down some part of her, the part that has to deal with the knowledge of her cheater husband, might be receptive to Grace's monologue about "trying")

# Being conscious of my face
(Morris, _Science of On-Camera Acting_)
* Reminded me of study where most emotions could be accurately delivered and interpreted by complete strangers by just looking at the eyes
* In my own daily life, I’ve been told I have “very expressive eyebrows.” I think this was super helpful in stage acting growing up, where expressions had to be done much more loudly for a large live audience. It was a pretty eye-opening exercise to consciously make myself relax my upper face when on camera. I felt and, when watching myself back on screen, saw expressions come out more through very subtle shifts in my gaze, mouth, cheeks, and the way I postured myself.
* The idea of less being more on camera really comes across watching my classmates as well. It’s really cool to be able to pinpoint when a character’s attitude or train of thought completely shifts from such hardly noticeable movement.
* It was also interesting to hear feedback on being hyperaware of our own micro-habits, like touching one's nose, nodding, or flicking eyebrows, as they are so greatly amplified through the camera
# Interacting with objects 
(_The Definitive Guide to Uta Hagen’s Acting Technique_)
* Visualizing objects when staring into audience or at blank walls – reminded me of a story my high school drama teacher told us about an actor (I don’t recall who or what production) who would practice staring at a tennis ball hung from the lighting rig to practice focusing on something suspended in midair, eventually being able to convince himself and everyone around him that the object was there even if it actually wasn’t during the shows, by just focusing his eyes at a point in midair
* Behavioral vs. Psychological images
