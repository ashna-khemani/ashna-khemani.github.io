Book: _Difficult Conversations_, by Stone, Patton, and Heen

- Difficult convos have a common structure if you pay attention to what’s NOT said…

	- Gap between said vs. not said makes it a difficult convo

# The 3 Conversations happening

## “What happened?”
* Disagreement on what has happened / should happen. Who’s to blame? What do we do now?
* Where most of the time is spent
### Truth
- Assumption: I'm right, you're wrong. Is this a fair assumption?
- Difficult convos are not about facts, they're about interpretation and action → what is **important** 
	- Ex: both agree the brochure is wrong, disagreement is whether it matters and how to handle it if so
- 💡 shift purpose from proving you're right → understand perception, interpretation, and values of both sides. Ask questions, how is everyone making sense of world.
	- → note that this is NOT "the truth"! people tend to get tangled in what they *perceive as "truth"*; not productive

### Intentions
* People assume others' intentions. Affects how you think of them → how convo goes
* Often don't know intentions, but often assume they're bad
* Intentions are invisible but important. Assuming them can make things hairy

### Blame
- Similar to arguing about the "truth." Brings more disagreement, denial, no learning. Fear of punishment, a binary answer
- No one wants to be blamed → everyone's energy goes into defending themselves
- Situation that led to the difficult convo is almost always result of what both parties did/failed to do
- Smartest move is to figure out:
	1) what prevented you from seeing it coming
	2) how to prevent it from happening again
* 💡 explore why things went wrong, how to correct going forward
* 💡 think "contribution," not "blame"


## “Feelings”
* Are my feelings valid, do I share them, what do I do about other person’s feelings? Are they hurt?
* May not be addressed directly but they leak in

## “Identity”

* What does situation mean to me, impact to my ego and self-image? Determines if you feel balanced or anxious
* Ultimately conversations feel difficult because something about you is at stake for you
* People tend to see traits as all or nothing, black and white






# Learning Stance
* Goal might shift from delivering message to sharing info, asking questions. From persuading to understanding other POV, explaining yours, and find a way to move fwd.
- People tend to think other people are the problem, irrational, naive, selfish, controlling, etc... People make sense to themselves
- Arguing = trading personal conclusions. But neither will make sense to the other → lack of understanding inhibits change
	- Many other layers we go through. Available info → observations → interpretations → conclusions
- Move from **certainty → curiosity**. What info does other person have that I don't?
- Consider your story; why do you believe what you believe? What is your POV?

# And Stance
- Accept the world is complex; you both can feel wronged and angry
- Understand each other, how can we manage this problem?

# Disentangle Intent from Impact
- We typically don't know what other people's intentions are, even if we think we do
- And we typically assume the worst, especially when tone isn't clear (ex: over text)
	- And treat ourselves more charitably...
- Leads to trap: assuming bad intentions mean bad character
- Also not worth trying to clarify intentions, because we skip over information that other person is trying to share
- What to ask yourself
	1) Actions: What did the other person say or do?
	2) Impact: What was the impact on me?
	3) Assumption: What assumption am I making about what the other person intended?
* ==> recognize it's an assumption! Can't get rid of it. Share what you assume about their intentions. Do they make sense to the other person

## Defensiveness is inevitable
* Clarify what you are trying to communicate or not
* Relieve the other person of feeling like they need to defend
* Listen for feelings
* Reflect on the complexity of your own intentions. Of course they were pure, but what exactly were you trying to do?

# Abandoning blame
* Inhibits ability to learn what's causing problem and doing anything to correct it
* Can't get away from blame entirely, but better alternative to think about is contribution

## Contribution vs. Blame
* Blame : judging : looking backward :: contribution : understanding : looking forward
* Contribution is joint. People tend to see problem as entirely one person's fault
* Blame kills understanding, hinders problem-solving. Can also hide deeper problems
### Hard-to-spot contributions
* **Avoiding** addressing the problem and letting it grow unchecked (ex: husband is always late)
* Being **unapproachable**
* **Intersections** – difference in background, preferences, communication style, assumptions about relationships... people tend to misinterpret this is "right and wrong" instead of an intersection of different people. Just need to compromise sometimes
* **Assumptions about roles**. Saying it's just the way it is (?)

### How to spot contributions
* Role reversal: what would they say I'm contributing? See yourself through other person's eyes
* Observer's insight: how does disinterested observer like a consultant view this?

## Shifting to contribution
* Map the contributions: what contributions has everyone made? You, them, anyone else?
* Make them understand their contributions. Share observations and reasoning clearly. Clarify what you'd have them do differently 



# Feelings
* People tend to try to frame them out of the conversation, even though they might be at the heart of what's happening, difficult to ignore.
* Emotions leak through even if you try not to clearly state them
* Listening ability often improves after sharing your own strong feelings, and feeling more heard

## Where do feelings hide?

### Emotional footprint
* How did family handle emotions, what was your role, what emotions do you find easy to acknowledge and express and with who? Which are harder?

- Accept that feelings are normal and natural
- Good people can have bad feelings like anger, failure, etc.
- Won't always be happy with what you feel. Will feel what you feel whether or not it makes sense; has no implication on whether you're a good person
- Sometimes honor others' feelings above your own for a variety of reasons. But don't ignore them bc shows others that they can also ignore your feelings → resentment grows

- Some emotions are harder to identify:

## Feelings in Attributions, Judgements, and Accusations
* The latter 3 are not feelings!
* "You are thoughtless" vs. "I feel hurt." Find feelings related to angry attributions/judgements/accusations
* Use the urge to blame as clue to find important feelings. Feeling of incompleteness until blame is assigned → should cue you to search for hidden feelings → shift to contribution


* **Negotiate with feelings**. Should try to share all feelings but don't have to share as it. Feelings based on perception and perception is negotiable. What story are you telling yourself, what might it be missing, and what could other person's story be?

## Don't Vent. Describe feelings carefully

1) Frame feelings back into problem
	- Don't feel bad that feelings are "irrational," won't change the fact that they're like that
	- Can preface that you're not comfortable with what you feel or unsure they make sense

2) Express the full spectrum
	- Being emotional ≠ expressing emotions
	- Express the full spectrum of feelings. Can lead to better understanding but wont make it easier. Can lead to more mutual support

3) Don't evaluate, just share
	- Avoid judgement, attribution, or blame
		- Can say "when this happened, I felt this way"
	- Don't monopolize. If you feel strongly, they probably do too. No feelings cancel
	- "I feel..."

# Identity
- Difficult conversations threaten identity. 3 cores
	- Am I competent
	- Am I a good person
	- Am I worthy of love
- "All or nothing" syndrome
	- Denial. We say, "they don't know what they're talking about"
	- Exaggeration. Letting feedback define who you are. Do well 99/100 times, but the 1/100 time you failed makes you feel like a little shit

## Ground your identity
1) Be aware of identity issues
	- What tends to knock you off balance, what about identity feels at risk?
	- What are you afraid of?
2) Complexify your identity; adopt the [[Difficult Conversations#And Stance|And Stance]] 
	- Avoid all-or-nothing identities
	- Have a self-image that allows for complexity. No bottom line for a character!


## 3 Things to Accept About Yourself
1) You will make mistakes
	* No all-or-nothing standards for yourself
	* If you make a mistake, admit it to yourself
2) Your intentions are complex
	- We may get nervous because we know past behavior was not motivated by good intentions
	- Be honest with yourself about the complexity. If you get accused of having bad intentions, you can still be on your feet, and [[Difficult Conversations#Regaining Balance|regain balance]]
3) You have contributed to the problem
	- What are things you've done that contributed to the problem
	- Again, not striking an all-or-nothing identity

## Regaining Balance
### Don't try to control their reaction
- Don't want them to feel bad. But you can't control that, and you shouldn't try.
	- If not (ex: "Everything will be fine") can come across as stifling or invalidating
- Have control over yourself and give them space to be honest in response (their feelings make sense and are ok to feel)
- Should not measure how successful conversation was based on if they were upset

### Prepare for Response
- Instead of focusing on how bad it can go → what can you learn about how they might response. Do these pose identity issues for you?
- How will you respond?

### Imagine it's 3mo or 10yr later
- Will you eventually feel better?
- Will it still seem important?
- Think if future self looking back on this. What do you think you'll have learned? How will you feel about how you handled it? What advice would future you give yourself now?

### Take a break
* Ask for time to think about what you've heard
* Check for distortions, denial, exaggerations.
* What's the worst that could happen
* What can you do to turn the conversation around
* It's ok to ask to postpone the conversation




# Create a Learning Conversation
- **To Raise or Not to Raise?** Is this the right time to raise the issue or leave it alone?
- Some conversations don't make sense. there are...

## 3 questions to ask yourself
1) Is the real conflict inside you?
2) Is there a better way to address the issue than talking about it? Take action to help both of you?
3) Do you have purposes that make sense? Don't focus on short-term relief at expense of long-term cost, like hurting other person back. Don't be passive-aggressive. If gonna talk about it, really talk about it, not just in passing.

## Letting go
- Adopt some assumptions to feel freer
1) It's not my responsibility to make things better; it's my responsibility to do my best
	- Don't be consumed by the "if only's"
2) They have limitations too
	- They know something bothers you but they don't remedy it. They are not perfect, or capacity to be different right now.
3) This conflict is not who I am
	- Step back and remember why you're fighting
	- Don't fight for the sake of keeping it going, because you feel it defines who you are/what you stand for
	- Fight for the end
4) Letting go ≠ not caring
	- Let go of emotions, and identity issues
	- Have sound [[Difficult Conversations#Purposes that work|purposes]]

## Purposes that work
1) Learning their story
2) Expressing your views and feelings
3) Problem-solving together

## The 3rd Story
- What a keen observer would tell, with no stake. A **mediator**
- Get 2 people with different stories to agree to the same description of what's happening
- "My sense is that you and I see this situation differently. I'd like to share how I see it, and learn more about how you're seeing it"

## Extend an Invitation
- I propose mutual understanding and problem-solving as purpose. Does this make sense to you? Let's have a conversation
- Describe your purposes. Make it clear their POV has a place, and you're not trying to change them
- An invitation can be turned down. Propose it as a joint task
- They're your partner in figuring it out
	- Sometimes, can share your internal struggle to cast them in more positive role. Can you help me get my perspective back, because I do not understand where you are coming from
- Be persistent. 

- Listening to others helps them listen to you. Hear and acknowledge their feelings
- Mindset should be "help me understand" instead of "I understand"
- Pay attention to your internal voice

- Inquire *only* to learn. 
	- Never dress assertion as question or do rhetorical questions
	- Don't cross-examine. "How do you explain..." Trying to persuade the person... wrong!
	- Ask open-ended questions. "Tell me more..." "Help me understand..."
- Make it safe for them to not answer
- Paraphrase for clarity
- Acknowledge feelings, Answer invisible questions
	- Ack ≠ agreement


# Expression
- You don't need to be an orator :)
- **You're entitled**, and everyone's feelings are equally important. Stand up for yourself

## Speak the heart of the matter. 
- What do you want to say?
	- Start with what matters to you the most
	- Don't rely on subtext, say what you mean
	- Avoid easing in. It increases both sides' anxiety and defensiveness. State thoughts outright and make it clear that you want to know if they see situation differently
	- Don't make your story simplistic. Can explain nuance and invite others to share or course-correct you

## Tell Story with Clarity
1) Don't present your conclusions as the truth. Avoid judgy statements
2) Share where your conclusions come from. Make it make sense
3) Don't exaggerate with superlatives, give them room to change. Things like "always" and "never" derail argument to frequency that thing happens. Makes it harder for people to change behavior.

## Help them Understand You
- Ask how they see it differently, and why. Avoid "Does that make sense/do you agree." Discover true reaction


# Problem-Solving – take the lead
## Reframe
- Each person thinks they have the truth → share perspectives
- Accusations → share how impacted feelings
- Blame → go over each other's contributions
- Judgements and characterizations → feelings
- Flaws → what's going on, how can we work together

## Listen
- Be persistent about listening. Not a passive role, can listen to direct convo.
- Listen for stories and feelings

## Make trouble explicit
- Name the dynamic (ex if they keep talking over you, getting angry, denying they're angry, etc.)
- Clears table for honest discussion

## Begin to Problem-Solve
- Need to compromise to make accommodations for others' needs
	- Don't get caught up in persuading them
- Be open to be persuaded, but be ready to live with no agreement if absolutely needed. Make this clear
- **→ you don't have to agree**
- Identify conflicting assumptions/hypothesis, discuss how to fairly test which assumption is empirically valid, or to what extent it is valid
- Say 
	- what is missing 
	- what would persuade you
	- what would persuade them?
	- what's their advice? What would they do if they were in your situation?
- **Persuasion is a two-way street**
- "Mutual caretaking" in coming up with solutions
- Consider alternatives if you can't agree
- Know that things take time. Difficult conversations are usually many over a period of time

# Put it all together!

1) Prepare. Walk through 3 Conversations
	- Shake your confidence a little → be open to be hearing what other has to say
	- Accepting contribution = more grounded = more confident. Each person's story matters
2) Check your purposes, decide whether to raise it. Do *you* think it's important? Remember your goal shouldn't be to control their reaction
3) Start from the [[Difficult Conversations#The 3rd Story|3rd story]]
4) Explore their story and yours
5) Problem-solving
